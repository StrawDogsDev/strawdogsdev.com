Three Flows for Greg’s Book

Same fuel, three routes to conviction. Each flow is a different answer to one question: in what order does the reader come to believe? Built against The Conviction Engine MS v7.5 and Master Canon Current State Draft 0.1 (Aug 8, 2026). Audience: leaders across industry and educational institutions.

The engine, and what it already told us

The manuscript is fuel-rich: 46,850 words carrying more validated facts than any single flow can burn. The strategy question is not which facts are true — the canon governs that — but which sequence of unanswered questions carries a reader from hearing to doing. That is a Flow problem, stage two of six, and it is the stage a chapter-order decision actually controls.

Formula 1
Story = Facts + Flow + Meaning

Curate the load-bearing facts, order them so the reader is always one unanswered question from the next line, then strike the spark.

Formula 2
Meaning = Conflict × Character × Change × Stakes

Multiplication, not addition. Any element at zero collapses the product. The June beta found the book’s signature blind spot: Stakes — brilliant diagnosis, costs in the aggregate, nobody bleeding on the page.

Formula 3
Drive = Can I do it × Will it work × Will it matter

Three doors, all belonging to the reader, all needing to open. A failed story makes a diagnosable sound: compliments mean door one, cross-examination door two, silence door three.

Three constraints every flow must satisfy

1. The ask never changes. All three flows end at the same action: run the ten-minute written-definitions diagnostic on one live initiative, then install from there. The master diagnostic — what do you want to do now? — has one answer. The flows differ only in the route to wanting it.

2. The incumbent must be displaced, not refuted. Every reader arrives running a story already. Each flow names a different incumbent and displaces it in a different place — that choice, more than any other, is what separates the three.

3. The two known engine gaps must be repaired in-flow. The Stakes blind spot (losses rendered diffuse and reversible) and the missing book-level Change case (no organization shown before-and-after) are inherited from the current draft. Each flow fixes both, differently.

The Diagnostic Arc — start where it hurts

Recognition first. The reader’s own Monday is the fuel; the theory arrives as the diagnosis of a pain already felt.
Incumbent story & displacement

“It’s a people problem.” Hire better, train harder, communicate more. An identity story — we’re doing it right and it’s still hard, so someone must be failing.

Displaced in Movement 2 by exoneration: thirty-five capable people, one instruction, thirty-five results, zero mistakes. The fault moves from character to conditions — a better story, not a rebuttal.

Identity recast (Emotion stage)

“I am not a failing leader. I am running an un-architected system — and I can be the one who architects it.”

Compressed identity for the reader: friction is a clue.

Flow 3

The Clinical Arc — show the diagnosis

Trust first. The reader watches a diagnostician work real cases until they can run the differential themselves; the theory arrives last, as the explanation of what they have already seen work.
Incumbent story & displacement

“Consultants sell frameworks.” A nothing-changes story about advice itself — the hardest incumbent in the set.

Displaced by posture, not argument: physicians evaluate before intervening, and this author opens with his own failed diagnosis. You cannot cross-examine a demonstration.

“I am the clinician of my own organization now. Evaluate before intervening is mine.”

Compressed identity: diagnose before you treat.

The trade-offs — pick what you can live with

There is no right strategy here. All three flows burn the same fuel to the same ask, and each one buys its conviction by giving something up. So the working question is not which is best but which set of costs are we prepared to accept? Below, each flow’s trade-offs are stated as terms — the things you are agreeing to live with if you choose it. Mark them as you go; the tallies keep score. This page is meant to be worked through with Greg, out loud.

How to choose

There is no right answer on this page — the real decision lives in the Trade-offs tab, where each flow’s costs are stated as terms to accept or decline. What this table adds is the audience lens: the choice is not which flow is best written — all three burn the same fuel to the same ask. The choice is which door Greg’s audience has shut hardest. If the numb-to-management-books executive is the primary reader, their shut door is will it matter, and Flow 1 opens it fastest. If the deans, boards, and institutions are the strategic prize, their shut door is is this legitimate, and Flow 2 is the only one built to open it. If the consultant-weary operator is the reader Greg most wants — and his renunciation suggests it is — their shut door is will this actually work or is it another framework, and Flow 3 is the only flow that never gives them a pitch to resist.

The flows also compose. Flow 1’s spine will accept Flow 2’s opening chapter and Flow 3’s mid-book diagnostic without breaking — that hybrid is roughly the August 5 architecture plus The Pattern plus one structural move. The maps keep the belief sequences separate so that if we hybridize, we do it deliberately, knowing which conviction route we are privileging and which failure sound to monitor.

One constant worth repeating to Greg: in all three flows the reader finishes wanting the same thing — ten minutes, one live initiative, written definitions, and the dispersion seen with their own eyes. The book’s ask never changes. Only the road to wanting it does.
